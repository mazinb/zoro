# When the AI Bubble Bursts, Who Will Be Left Holding the Bag?

**August 12, 2026 · 10 min read**

---

The numbers are staggering. In 2025 alone, four hyperscalers -- Amazon, Google, Meta, and Microsoft -- collectively pledged approximately $400 billion in AI spending, with capital expenditures hitting a record $320 billion according to Forbes. OpenAI committed $300 billion in cloud computing capacity from Oracle over five years starting in 2027, averaging $60 billion per year. Goldman Sachs warned back in 2024 that roughly $1 trillion would be spent on AI infrastructure "with little to show for it so far." These are not startup checks written by enthusiastic angels. These are balance-sheet decisions by the world's largest technology companies, backed by decades of competitive paranoia.

What makes this moment historically significant is the sheer scale of the commitment relative to revenue. OpenAI generated approximately $20 billion in revenue in 2025 but committed to $1.4 trillion in infrastructure spending over eight years. Oracle is already incurring a $100 million loss renting data centers to OpenAI. CoreWeave, an AI infrastructure company that had its revenue doubling to $2.58 billion in Q2 2026, still lost $740 million in 90 days. The structure is a game of musical chairs, but the music is playing at a volume that might drown out everyone in the room.

## The Scale of the Spending Problem

The central tension in the current AI investment environment is simple to state and impossible to resolve: spending is outpacing revenue by an order of magnitude, and the gap is widening. The numbers paint a picture that should be familiar to anyone who studied the dot-com crash of 2000.

In 2025, hyperscaler capital expenditures hit a record $320 billion, according to Forbes and Peter Cohan's analysis. OpenAI's deal with Oracle alone commits $300 billion over five years, starting in 2027. This is not a speculative investment in a promising technology. This is infrastructure spending at a scale that suggests the buyers believe they are building the foundation of a new economic era. Whether they are right depends entirely on what happens next.

The spending does not stop at infrastructure. Goldman Sachs, in their 2024 analysis, estimated roughly $1 trillion would be spent on AI infrastructure with "little to show for it so far." That phrase matters. The technology is advancing, certainly. But revenue is the only metric that determines whether a company can sustain its spending trajectory. OpenAI generated approximately $20 billion in revenue in 2025. Its projected revenue was expected to reach $13 billion in 2025, requiring even larger amounts to cover shortfalls. The gap between $20 billion in revenue and $300 billion in committed cloud computing is a chasm that subscription plans are unlikely to bridge.

CoreWeave's financial performance illustrates the problem in sharper relief. Revenue doubled to $2.58 billion in Q2 2026, yet the company lost $740 million in those same 90 days. Oracle, which lost $100 million in its data center rental business to OpenAI, demonstrates that even the infrastructure layer of the AI ecosystem is not immune to the spending problem.

## The Startup Valuation Crash

While hyperscalers burn cash on infrastructure, the broader startup ecosystem is experiencing a valuations correction that could prove devastating for many companies. The data is unambiguous.

According to CNBC in June 2026, more than 220 former unicorns have lost their billion-dollar valuations as AI booms redirected venture capital capital away from traditional startup categories. The Next Web reported that startups that last raised in 2021 -- the peak of the pre-AI funding environment -- are now worth 68% less on average, with software-as-a-service firms hit the hardest.

The mechanism is straightforward. AI attracted approximately 80% of global venture funding in Q1 2026, but nearly 65% of that went to just four companies: Anthropic, OpenAI, Waymo, and xAI. Pre-ChatGPT firms were effectively cut off from venture funding because their valuations were inflated and their technology was deemed outdated. This created a feedback loop where non-AI startups could not raise at reasonable valuations, which made them less attractive to investors, which further depressed valuations, which made it harder to raise -- a death spiral for thousands of companies that have nothing to do with artificial intelligence.

The concentration of capital in the AI space means that even within the AI bubble, most startups are getting nothing. The 220 former unicorns that lost their billion-dollar status include many that are now desperate for capital that simply does not exist. When the music stops, these companies do not get to keep their valuations. They get liquidated.

## The Circular Financing Web

One reason the AI bubble might be more dangerous than the dot-com bubble is the circular nature of the financing. Nvidia invests in labs that buy Nvidia GPUs, which then generate revenue for Nvidia. This creates a closed loop where money circulates within the ecosystem without necessarily creating corresponding value in the broader economy.

Wikipedia's AI bubble article from August 2026 documents how AI vendors fund data center buildouts for each other. The structure looks different from dot-com, and arguably more risky, because the circularity is embedded in the supply chain itself. During the dot-com era, investment banks funded Internet startups through IPO proceeds, but the technology itself was not part of the financing mechanism. Today, the equipment, the software, the infrastructure, and the end products are all financed through overlapping commitments that depend on each other remaining solvent.

Andrew.ooo's July 2026 analysis of the dot-com comparison highlights this distinction. The similarities are striking: concentration risk, circular financing, valuation multiples of 30 to 50 times revenue, and elevated insider selling. The differences are also important: Nvidia has real revenue projected at $120 billion for 2025, the technology has real productive applications, the IPO market has been restrained, and the Federal Reserve has been accommodative. The key question is whether these differences are sufficient to prevent a significant market correction.

## Who Gets Hurt Most

If the AI bubble bursts, the damage will not be evenly distributed. Some players will absorb the losses. Others will emerge intact. The likely casualties fall into several categories.

Single-tenant data center REITs represent the most exposed segment. These are companies where one hyperscaler is responsible for 70% or more of the revenue. If that hyperscaler reduces its capital expenditure, the REIT's value collapses. This creates a transmission mechanism: hyperscaler spending cuts flow directly into REIT defaults, which flow into financial institutions that held REIT debt, which then affects the broader market.

Pre-revenue AI startups at Series C and beyond represent another casualty group. Down rounds are accelerating in Q3 and Q4 2026. Companies that raised heavily on optimistic AI revenue projections will find those projections untenable. Their valuations will crash, their investors will demand more equity, and many will fail entirely.

Memory suppliers with patent overhang -- Kioxia, Samsung, and Western Digital -- face a different but equally serious threat. If AI spending declines, demand for high-bandwidth memory chips drops dramatically, and these companies have significant debt from capacity expansion.

Chip stocks with concentrated hyperscaler exposure follow the same pattern. Nvidia's trajectory, while strong today, depends on hyperscalers continuing their spending. If they cut back, Nvidia's revenue falls.

AI-linked ETFs compound the problem by construction. They concentrate top-AI-name exposure, meaning the crash in individual AI stocks translates directly into portfolio losses for millions of retail investors who bought into the narrative without understanding the concentration risk.

## Signs of Recalibration

The bubble might not burst in the dramatic, all-at-once way that the dot-com crash did. Several signs point toward a more gradual recalibration.

Global venture funding has dropped sharply. Fewer AI startups are being born, according to a December 2025 Medium article. The funding peak was in 2024, and the decline since then suggests that investors are becoming more selective. The January 2026 claim that 98% of AI startups will fail over a few month period is extreme, but the sentiment is real: the easy money is gone.

Analysts are starting to warn that "market expectations may be far ahead of what the technology can deliver," according to a TechTarget piece from 2026. Reddit's r/ExperiencedDevs thread in April 2026 noted that the bubble already burst in 2025, with first-generation small AI startups going out of business.

The counterargument to the bubble thesis is compelling. Nvidia's revenue is real. The productive applications of AI -- from drug discovery to material science to software development -- are genuinely transformative. The Federal Reserve's accommodative stance provides liquidity that the dot-com era lacked. And the restrained IPO market means that the speculative frenzy that drove dot-com valuations to absurd levels has not fully materialized in the public markets.

The difference between the AI bubble and the dot-com bubble is the same as the difference between a controlled descent and a crash. One is a process. The other is an event. Understanding which one we are experiencing determines the strategy.

## What Comes Next

If the AI bubble deflates gradually, the survivors will be clear. Hyperscalers with real SaaS revenue -- Microsoft Azure with M365 Copilot, Google Cloud, AWS -- have the diversified income streams that can sustain them through a downturn. Nvidia, unless Blackwell orders are cancelled, has the market position and the technological moat that justify its valuation.

AI subscription revenue at OpenAI and Anthropic -- ChatGPT Pro and Claude Pro -- do not correlate with public equity moves, suggesting that the consumer market is developing independently of the speculative investment cycle. Enterprise AI vendors with signed multi-year contracts -- Palantir, ServiceNow, Salesforce -- have the revenue visibility that makes them resilient regardless of market conditions.

The key insight is this: the bubble burst in dot-com wiped out entire categories of companies, but the infrastructure -- fiber optic cables, routers, data centers -- remained. The productive assets survived even when the valuations did not. The same is likely true for AI. The chips, the models, the infrastructure will continue to exist and generate value. The companies that overpaid for them may not.

The question for investors, founders, and workers is not whether AI is valuable -- it is -- but whether the current valuation structure can sustain itself. The answer, based on the spending-to-revenue ratio, the concentration of capital, the circular financing patterns, and the valuation corrections already underway, is that some form of reckoning is almost inevitable. The question is when, how, and who is left holding the bag.

The timeline matters enormously. A gradual deflation gives companies time to adjust. A sudden crash forces fire sales and mass layoffs. The dot-com bubble took three years to reach its trough after the peak, but the damage was concentrated in the first 18 months. AI is playing out at a much faster pace, with quarterly earnings reports providing real-time updates on spending versus revenue. This speed creates both opportunity and risk for those who understand the dynamics.

## Key Takeaways

- AI infrastructure spending has reached $400 billion in a single year from hyperscalers alone, with spending far exceeding revenue.
- Pre-ChatGPT startups have lost an average of 68% of their valuation as venture capital concentrated in four AI companies.
- Circular financing between chip makers, data center operators, and AI labs creates systemic risk that extends beyond the technology sector.
- Single-tenant REITs, pre-revenue Series C+ startups, and memory suppliers are the most exposed to a downturn.
- Hyperscalers with real SaaS revenue, Nvidia, and enterprise AI vendors with multi-year contracts are positioned to survive any correction.
