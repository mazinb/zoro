# Zen and the Art of Surviving India's Startup Crash

## The Crash That Shook India

Bangalore's co-working spaces hummed with a manic energy that felt like the future itself was being built one pitch deck at a time. In 2021, India's startup ecosystem was on top of the world. The country hosted the most unicorns globally, with 44 new unicorns created that year alone. Total funding for Indian startups exceeded $23 billion, making 2021 a banner year for entrepreneurship [2][3]. The narrative was unstoppable: India had a demographic dividend, a growing digital economy, and a generation of ambitious founders building companies at an unprecedented scale.

But behind the glamour, cracks were forming. A wave of corporate governance failures exposed by the Byju's saga revealed the dark side of the boom. Byju's, India's most valuable startup at a $22 billion valuation, was accused of massive financial irregularities [10]. The Indian Institute of Management Bangalore expelled Byju Raveendran from its alumni association over the scandal [10]. Former employees of Byju's alleged unpaid taxes, ghost employees on payroll, and a toxic work culture [12]. The company was founded by Byju Raveendran, a former math tutor who built an edtech empire that went public on the Australian stock exchange : the kind of story that made everyone believe anything was possible.

Meanwhile, in the broader ecosystem, the pattern of overvaluation was becoming clear. According to Startup Chai, an analysis by venture capitalist Gaurang Shah revealed that VCs were inflating valuations through competitive bidding [4]. The average valuation of Indian startups in 2021 was a staggering 354x sales. For context, the top 50 U.S. startups at the same time averaged 40x sales. This massive disparity was not a coincidence; it was the result of a VC arms race where firms competed to back the next unicorn by offering sky-high valuations.

But the consequences of this overvaluation were swift and brutal. Once the funding winter hit in 2022, startups that had been valued at billion-dollar levels in 2021 saw their valuations collapse by 90 percent or more. The zombie startups that existed solely on the basis of inflated valuations began to die. By 2026, nearly a quarter of Indian startups were classified as zombies, with Rs 5 lakh crore (approximately $60 billion) wiped from the sector's total valuation [11].

The human toll was significant. Thousands of startup employees lost their jobs. Office spaces that were once bustling with hundreds of young professionals became empty. The psychological toll on founders who had bet everything on their companies was substantial.

## The Sequoia Effect: When the Gatekeepers Blink

Perhaps the most telling moment in India's startup crash was not a bankruptcy filing or a layoff announcement, but a corporate name change. In 2023, Sequoia India, the iconic venture capital firm that had been the gold-standard backer for Indian startups, announced it would split from its parent company and rebrand as Peak XV Partners. This was widely interpreted as Sequoia distancing itself from the Indian startup ecosystem after a series of high-profile failures [6][8].

Sequoia had become synonymous with validation in the Indian startup world. Getting funding from Sequoia was considered a seal of quality, a signal to other investors that the startup was worth backing. When Sequoia walked away, it sent shockwaves through the ecosystem. The message was clear: even the most experienced investor in India's startup scene had gotten it wrong.

This was a pivotal moment. For years, Indian founders and employees had used Sequoia's involvement as a proxy for company quality. If Sequoia was investing, the company must be good. If Sequoia was walking away, there was a problem. The rebrand to Peak XV was symbolic of the end of an era: the era of unquestioned faith in the Indian startup narrative.

This ripple effect had broader implications. Other established VCs became more cautious. New entrants to the Indian VC scene paused or reversed their plans. The cost of capital for startups increased dramatically. What had been a seller's market became a buyer's market, with investors holding all the leverage.

## The Layoffs: A Tsunami of Terminations

As the funding winter deepened, layoffs became the new normal. In December 2022, the IANS/ETHRWorld reported that layoffs and the funding winter were set to continue into 2023 [5]. The term "cockroaches, not unicorns" entered the startup lexicon, popularized by Rushit Shah, who argued that the startups that would survive the crash would be the resilient, adaptable ones, not the overvalued ones with inflated egos.

The wave of terminations swept through thousands of employees across the ecosystem. Startups that had been aggressively hiring just months earlier were suddenly closing offices and letting go of staff. The psychological impact on employees was profound. Many had joined startups with the promise of equity and rapid career growth. When the funding stopped, those promises evaporated.

The fallout of the crash was significant. While the headlines focused on founder fortunes and VC losses, the real victims were the employees who had bet their careers on companies that collapsed. Many found themselves unemployed in a job market that was suddenly flooded with experienced startup professionals.

Rushit Shah's "cockroaches" analogy captured the essential truth of the crash: survival, not glamour, was what mattered. The cockroaches would survive. The unicorns would perish. And the ecosystem would be better for it.

## Signs of Life: The Recovery Is Real But Uneven

By 2024, signs of recovery began to appear. According to Inc42's Indian Startup Funding Report 2024, total funding across reported deals declined by approximately 8 percent year-over-year to $11 billion, but the decline was significantly smaller than the 78 percent collapse seen in 2023 [13]. 2024 saw record-breaking IPOs, with Swiggy's $1.35 billion IPO as a highlight [14]. Indian startups collectively raised $16.11 billion when including PIPEs and secondary transactions, according to alternative methodology used by Inc42 [13].

A particularly encouraging trend: women-led startups showed remarkable strength, raising $930 million across 136 deals in 2024, representing 93.75 percent growth from 2023's $480 million [7]. Fintech and e-commerce led the way among women-founded companies. This is more than a feel-good statistic. Startups founded by women tend to be more capital-efficient and profitable, according to multiple studies of the global venture scene. In India's context, women founders are building companies that survive the funding winter rather than chasing unicorn status.

Artha VCs sentiment report suggested the funding winter was nearing a turning point, with early-stage funding picking up [13]. Early-stage investment is often the leading indicator of recovery, as it reflects investor confidence in the next generation of founders rather than the survivors of the old guard.

However, the recovery has been deeply uneven. While early-stage funding is picking up, late-stage funding remains cautious. The number of new unicorns created in 2024 has been modest compared to the 44 created in 2021. Mumbai overtook Bengaluru as the top startup funding city, suggesting a geographic shift in the ecosystem's center of gravity [13].

The central question is whether this recovery is structural or cyclical. The Secretariat's analysis argued that the current turmoil needs to be properly evaluated to ensure the nascent sector survives through these challenging times [1]. They noted that India's digital startups have historically shown pronounced business cycles where any slump is followed by a quick high, but asked: will this funding winter be over soon, followed by another high?

That answer may depend on whether the ecosystem has truly internalized the lessons of the crash, or whether the next cycle of easy money will produce the same overvaluation patterns that caused the crash in the first place.

Yet the crash is not entirely negative. The collapse of zombie startups, whose valuations existed purely on hope rather than fundamentals, was a necessary purge. A market that rewards companies generating nothing but PowerPoint slides is not a market at all. It is a casino. The fact that India's startup ecosystem is now forcing companies to prove they can generate revenue, retain customers, and build sustainable unit economics. That is a sign of maturation, not decay. The founders who are still standing have something the unicorns never had: a real business that earns its keep. The VCs who are still writing checks are doing so because they see a path to returns, not because they are caught in a competitive bidding frenzy. The survivors of the crash are not the cockroaches. They are the ones who never stopped eating in the first place.

## The VC Arms Race: How Competition Became Cancer

The overvaluation of Indian startups was not an accident. It was the result of a structural problem in the venture capital ecosystem. According to Gaurang Shah's analysis on Startup Chai, VCs were not just passively investing in Indian startups; they were actively inflating valuations through competitive bidding [4].

The mechanics were straightforward. When multiple VC firms were competing to back the same startup, they would bid up the valuation. The startup that secured the highest valuation would then use that valuation to justify raising more money at even higher levels. This created a self-reinforcing cycle of inflation.

Consider the math: if a startup raised $10 million at a $50 million valuation, then the next round at a $100 million valuation, and the next at $200 million, the company was now running at a $200 million valuation. If the company was generating only $1 million in revenue, the price-to-sales ratio was 200x. For comparison, the top 50 U.S. startups at the same time averaged 40x sales. The average valuation of Indian startups in 2021 was a staggering 354x sales.

This was not a sustainable model. When the funding environment tightened, the valuations had to come down. The question was not if, but how. The answer was: violently. Startups that had been valued at $500 million in 2021 found themselves worth less than $50 million by 2023. The write-downs were massive, and they created a wealth effect that rippled through the entire ecosystem.

The VC arms race also had a human cost. Founders became addicted to the validation that came with high valuations. Employees became dependent on the promise of equity that was no longer worth anything. Investors became trapped in the portfolios of companies whose valuations were now deeply underwater.

The lesson is clear: a market without competition produces better outcomes than a market in a competitive frenzy. The VCs that survived the crash were the ones that learned to resist the urge to bid up valuations. The founders who survived were the ones who focused on building real businesses rather than chasing valuation numbers.

## The Zen of Survival: What It Takes to Last

Surviving India's startup crash requires a fundamental rethinking of what startup success means. The old playbook, raise big, grow fast, exit early, is broken. The new playbook, build real products, achieve unit economics, grow sustainably, is harder but more durable.

For founders, the lesson is clear. Reducing the burn happened to be the new mantra, Rushit Shah observed [5]. Ambitious projects must be deprioritized. Survival comes before growth. But this does not mean playing small. The founders who will thrive in the next cycle are those who build companies with real fundamentals, not those who chase valuation numbers.

For investors, the lesson is equally clear. The era of writing checks without due diligence is over. The quality of deal flow matters more than the volume. As Rushit Shah put it, mostly the serious funding houses will prevail while we will see visitors in the VC world take a break [5].

For employees, the lesson is one of caution. The golden era of ESOP windfalls and rapid promotion tracks is over. Joining a startup in 2025 means joining a company that needs to earn its survival, not one that will inevitably reach unicorn status. Due diligence on your employer matters as much as due diligence on a company you might invest in.

India has experienced experienced one of the most dramatic boom-and-bust cycles in global entrepreneurial history. The crash was brutal but necessary. The overvaluation was real. The governance failures were real. The human cost was very real. But if the ecosystem learns from this crash, as The Secretariat suggested, the next funding upcycle can be more balanced and stable [1].

The zen of surviving India's startup crash is not about avoiding storms. It is about learning to sail in them. The cockroaches, as Shah called them, will survive. The unicorns that were merely paper wealth will perish. What emerges on the other side will be stronger, more resilient, and more real.
