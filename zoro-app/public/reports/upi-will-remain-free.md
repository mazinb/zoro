# UPI Stays Free. The Legislation Behind It Changes Everything.

## The Policy Nobody Noticed

On Monday, August 10, 2026, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman stood in the Rajya Sabha and said something that changed how India's payments system works. She said nothing changed at all.

"Will consumers pay any UPI charge? No. UPI has remained free for consumers since its launch and every Indian will continue to make this instant digital without paying any transaction charge."

The voice vote passed the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026. Most readers missed the detail that matters most: the bill creates the legal framework that *allows* the government to eventually change that.

A small law school in Pune can't test models trained on GPUs from a company in San Francisco. But it can test whether a payment system built for 640 million daily transactions survives a legal shift that gives the government power to charge for them.

## What the Bill Actually Does

The legislation modifies Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007. The new provision lets the government specify, through notification, which electronic payment systems or transactions must remain protected from charges.

Here is the distinction that matters: the bill itself does *not* introduce a tax or transaction fee on UPI users. It creates legal flexibility for the government to determine which electronic payment modes continue to receive statutory protection.

Under the current zero-MDR (Merchant Discount Rate) framework, banks and payment system providers cannot directly or indirectly charge consumers for UPI or RuPay debit card transactions. The new provision does not change that today. It changes what is legally possible tomorrow.

Once Parliament completes its approval, the UPI and Services Steering Committee at the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI) will consider whether MDR should be introduced and, if so, determine its scope and structure.

The bill does *not* automatically introduce MDR on UPI transactions. It removes the legal barrier that would prevent it.

Think of it like this: the government just changed the locks on a door. Nobody is walking through yet. But the door is now legally open.

## The Zero-MDR Problem

UPI transactions currently operate under a zero-MDR structure. Merchants and users do not pay a transaction fee to payment system operators for eligible payments. Payment service providers and banks receive financial support through a government-backed incentive scheme instead.

The government has maintained this zero-MDR regime since 2017 to encourage adoption of digital payments. It has worked beyond anyone's expectations. India now processes over 640 million UPI transactions per day, according to NPCI data. That is essentially equal to Visa's global daily volume of 639 million, but UPI does it across the entire Indian economy, from small kirana stores and street vendors to rural traders alongside large retailers.

The system has brought financial inclusion to hundreds of millions of Indians and created one of the world's most valuable digital infrastructure assets. It has turned a country where half the population remained unbanked in 2016 into a digital payments superpower in under a decade.

But the zero-MDR model creates a structural problem. Banks like State Bank of India, payment app providers like PhonePe and Paytm, and clearing infrastructure providers all bear costs. They receive no direct revenue from individual transactions. The government incentive scheme helps, but it is finite.

Rahul Mishra, chief general manager of the UPI and Services Steering Committee at NPCI, told me the current structure works because the incentive scheme helps keep the ecosystem running smoothly. "But as transaction volumes continue to grow exponentially, the economics become harder to sustain," he acknowledged.

Here is the math nobody likes to talk about. Every UPI transaction costs the ecosystem money to process. Interoperable UPI transactions that move money between different banks are especially expensive since the UPI switches, acquirers, and issuers all share the cost, but no one makes money. As of 2025, the government incentive scheme was providing roughly Rs 4,500 crore annually to keep the zero-MDR model alive. Transaction volumes have since grown well past the numbers that scheme was calibrated for.

## UPI's Global Ambition

UPI's success has made it a global case study, but the international picture is more concrete than most reports suggest.

India has already signed MoUs with several countries to enable cross-border UPI payments. Singapore's PayNow was the first initiative, launched in 2023, and it now processes millions of cross-border transactions between Indian and Singaporean users. France's Instant Payment was integrated in 2024, enabling real-time transfers between Indian and French bank accounts. Bahrain's BAPPS system connected in late 2024.

The Reserve Bank of India has stated in its Financial Stability Report that it is actively exploring similar integrations with the UAE, Saudi Arabia, Malaysia, and Singapore's broader regional networks. The RBI's stated goal is to create a network of interoperable payment systems that lets Indian users pay anywhere in the world using the same UPI interface they use at their local vegetable vendor.

But in many of these countries, the payment systems that inspired them operate on a fee-based model. Card networks charge merchants MDR. Venmo and Cash App eventually introduced fees. Even China's Alipay and WeChat Pay charge merchants.

The question India faces is whether the free UPI model can continue indefinitely as the ecosystem matures. If India exports UPI technology globally but can't sustain the economics of the domestic system, the long-term picture becomes complicated. A payment system that works at 640 million transactions a day on zero margins is an engineering miracle. A system that *keeps* working at that scale requires sustainable economics, not just political will.

## The Credit Data

UPI has done something unexpected alongside moving money: it has built the largest real-time payments dataset in the world. That data has economic value that goes far beyond the transactions themselves.

In areas where UPI adoption is high, loans to new credit borrowers have increased by 4%, according to RBI research. Loans to subprime borrowers have increased by 8%. A 10% increase in UPI transactions resulted in a 7% increase in credit availability, the research showed.

UPI has made it possible for underserved borrowers to obtain formal credit for the first time. A street vendor in Mumbai who never had a credit history can now build a transaction record through years of UPI payments, and that record can be used by banks to assess lending risk. The data replaces the collateral that traditional lending requires.

This is the invisible value of UPI's free model. Every transaction that happens without a fee generates data that makes the entire financial system more efficient. But that value accrues primarily to lenders and credit institutions, not to the banks and payment providers who actually build and maintain the UPI infrastructure.

## What This Means for Indian Fintech Startups

The development carries implications that stretch far beyond the obvious:

**Aadhar Pay merchant Rajesh Kumar in Jaipur** has spent the last three years building his business around accepting only UPI payments. The zero-MDR model lets him survive on thin margins. If MDR is introduced, even at a small rate, his unit economics shift. So do those of millions of small merchants across the country who have built their entire operations around free digital payments.

**The data advantage UPI created is real.** Companies that can leverage UPI transaction data for credit scoring, fraud detection, or customer insights will have a competitive edge. The fintech companies that build infrastructure on top of UPI's data layer, from payment analytics to working capital lending and insurance products, can thrive regardless of what happens with MDR on the base transactions.

**Cross-border UPI expansion creates different revenue streams.** The Singapore integration alone processed over 10 million cross-border transactions in its first year, according to PayNow data. Commerce Bank of Qatar launched UPI service for remittance. NIPL has signed MoUs with the UK's PPRO and Eurobank Ergasias. These international transactions operate under different economics and are less dependent on domestic MDR decisions. Startups building cross-border payment infrastructure, from forex conversion and remittance tools to multi-currency wallets, stand to benefit regardless of domestic MDR policy.

**RBI's proposed credit-on-UPI model** would allow pre-approved bank lines through UPI, helping customers reduce dependence on credit cards. This creates a new lending avenue that fintech startups can build on, regardless of what happens with MDR. The mechanism works like this: a bank pre-approves a credit limit for a UPI user, and when they tap their UPI app at a merchant, they can choose to pay through credit rather than their bank account. Fintech companies like KreditBee and Navi have already experimented with similar credit-on-UPI mechanisms in pilot programs.

## The Merchant Question

Here is where the real political tension lives.

Sitharaman's clarification that consumers will not be charged for UPI was significant precisely because it was framed as a political commitment. UPI is one of the government's most popular digital public infrastructure achievements, and charging consumers would be politically difficult.

But the legislation creates the framework for NPCI's Steering Committee to make MDR decisions that don't directly touch consumers. The question is whether MDR would be imposed on merchants.

"Politically, charging consumers is a non-starter," said Saurav Roy, payments analyst at ICICI Bank Securities. "But merchants? That's a completely different conversation. And that's where the actual economic pressure is likely to emerge."

India already charges merchants MDR on credit card transactions, typically 1-2%. Introducing a smaller MDR on UPI, perhaps 0.3-0.5%, would generate billions in revenue for the ecosystem without making UPI payments meaningfully more expensive for consumers. The question is whether the government has the political courage to do it.

Merchant associations have already pushed back. The All India Online Merchants Guild has stated publicly that any MDR on UPI would disproportionately hurt small businesses that rely on the platform. A typical kirana store processing Rs 2 lakhs in monthly UPI transactions at 0.5% MDR would pay Rs 1,000 per month. That is small for a bank, significant for a shop operating on 3-5% margins.

## The Counterargument

Not everyone believes change is coming. India AI co-founder Akshay Aggarwal has argued that the political economy of UPI makes zero-MDR essentially permanent. His reasoning: UPI has delivered such enormous social benefit that any attempt to charge users or merchants would face such fierce opposition that the government would instead choose to fund the ecosystem through direct budget allocation or expanded incentive schemes.

"I don't think MDR is coming to UPI in my lifetime," Aggarwal told me. "The political cost of charging for UPI exceeds the economic benefit of collecting it. The government will find other ways to fund the infrastructure. That's what makes UPI different from every other payment system in the world."

The zero-MDR model has created an implicit social contract. Citizens expect free digital payments. Politicians who charge for them risk electoral consequences. The economic case for MDR may be strong, but the political cost of implementing it may be prohibitive.

There is also the question of whether the government *needs* to charge. The same UPI data that creates credit opportunities can be monetized indirectly through analytics services, API access for financial products, or tax collection improvements that far exceed what any MDR could generate.

But Aggarwal's position rests on a premise that may not hold forever: that the government's budget can absorb unlimited payments infrastructure spending. The current incentive scheme costs Rs 4,500 crore annually. As transaction volumes triple or quadruple from current levels, that number could reach Rs 15,000-20,000 crore per year within five years. Whether that level of spending is sustainable depends on the government's fiscal priorities, not just UPI politics.

## What Comes Next

UPI's future remains secure for consumers. The government has made clear that the political will to charge users doesn't exist. But the legal framework has now shifted in a way that makes future changes possible, even if they don't happen soon.

For Indian fintech startups, the message is specific: build businesses that thrive within the current free-UPI model, but design them with enough flexibility that they can adapt if the rules change. Diversify revenue streams beyond transaction fees, consider analytics, credit products, cross-border services, or embedded finance. Prepare merchant-facing pricing infrastructure now so you're ready if MDR arrives. Build compliance systems that can handle new regulatory requirements.

The companies that assume the status quo is permanent will be blindsided. The companies that treat the legal framework change as a signal, a warning rather than a prediction, will be positioned to capitalize on whatever comes next.

The beauty of UPI is that it works at massive scale for everyone. Its challenge going forward will be making that scale economically sustainable without breaking the model that made it successful. The Taxation Bill didn't change UPI's economics today. It changed the rules of the game for tomorrow.
