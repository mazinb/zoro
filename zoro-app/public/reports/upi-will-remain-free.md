# UPI Will Remain Free: For Now. Here's What Changes.

## The Policy That Almost Nobody Noticed

On Monday, August 10, 2026, the Rajya Sabha passed the Taxation and Other Laws (Amendment) Bill, 2026 through a voice vote following a brief discussion. The legislation was presented by Union Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman, who took the opportunity to make one of the most important clarifications for India's digital economy:

"Will consumer pay any UPI charge? No. UPI has remained free for consumers since its launch and every Indian will continue to make this instant digital without paying any transaction charge."

On the surface, this is a reassurance. UPI stays free for consumers. What most observers missed is that the legislation actually creates the legal framework that *allows* the government to eventually change that.

## What the Bill Actually Does

The Taxation Bill, which was cleared by the Lok Sabha last week, modifies Section 10A of the Payment and Settlement Systems Act, 2007. The provision gives the government the ability to specify, through a notification, which electronic payment systems or transactions must remain protected from charges.

Here is the key distinction: the legislation itself does *not* introduce a tax or transaction fee on UPI users. It creates the legal flexibility for the government to determine which electronic payment modes continue to receive statutory protection from charges.

At present, banks and payment system providers cannot directly or indirectly charge consumers for transactions conducted through UPI or RuPay debit cards under the existing zero-MDR framework. The new provision doesn't change that today, but it changes what is legally possible tomorrow.

Once Parliament passes the legislation, the UPI and Services Steering Committee, headed by the National Payments Corporation of India (NPCI), will consider whether Merchant Discount Rate (MDR) should be introduced and, if so, determine its scope and structure.

This means the passage of the Bill does *not* automatically introduce MDR on UPI transactions. But it does remove the legal barrier that would prevent it.

## The Zero-MDR Paradox

To understand why this matters, you need to understand how UPI is funded today.

UPI transactions currently operate under a zero-MDR structure. Merchants and users do not pay a transaction fee to payment system operators for eligible UPI payments. Payment service providers and banks instead receive financial support through a government-backed incentive scheme.

The government has maintained this zero-MDR regime to encourage adoption of digital payments. It has worked spectacularly well. India now processes over 640 million UPI transactions per day, compared to 639 million by Visa globally. The system has brought financial inclusion to hundreds of millions of Indians and created one of the world's most valuable digital infrastructure assets.

But the zero-MDR model creates a structural problem. The companies that build, maintain, and support the UPI ecosystem (banks, payment app providers, clearing infrastructure) all bear costs. They receive no direct revenue from individual transactions. The government incentive scheme helps, but it is finite.

As transaction volumes continue to grow exponentially, the economics become harder to sustain.

## The Global Context

UPI's success has made it a global case study. Countries including Brazil, Bahrain, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, the United States, and the European Union are exploring options to implement a UPI-like system in their own domestic markets. The expansion of UPI demonstrates that the government is ensuring the technology it creates for citizens is easy to use, easily accessible, and constantly improved.

But in many of these countries, the payment systems that inspired them operate on a fee-based model. Card networks charge merchants MDR. Venmo and Cash App eventually introduced fees. Even China's Alipay and WeChat Pay charge merchants.

The question India faces is whether the free UPI model can continue indefinitely as the ecosystem matures. If India exports UPI technology globally but can't sustain the economics of the domestic system, the long-term picture becomes complicated.

## What This Means for Indian Fintech Startups

For the Indian fintech ecosystem, this development carries several important implications:

**UPI-dependent business models need to plan for change.** Companies whose entire economics rely on zero-fee UPI transactions need contingency plans. If MDR is introduced, even at a small rate, the unit economics of many business models will shift.

**New revenue opportunities are emerging.** Even with free UPI, the ecosystem has created enormous data and infrastructure advantages. UPI has made it possible for underserved borrowers to obtain formal credit for the first time. In areas where UPI adoption is high, loans to new credit borrowers have increased by 4%, while loans to subprime borrowers have increased by 8%. A 10% increase in UPI transactions resulted in a 7% increase in credit availability.

**Cross-border UPI is the next frontier.** UPI has been integrated with Singapore's PayNow. Commerce Bank of Qatar launched UPI service for remittance. NIPL has signed MoUs with the UK's PPRO and Eurobank Ergasias. The global expansion of UPI acceptance creates new revenue opportunities that are less dependent on domestic MDR decisions.

**The credit-on-UPI space is opening up.** RBI proposed allowing credit on UPI through pre-approved bank lines, helping customers reduce dependence on credit cards. This creates a new lending avenue that fintech startups can build on, regardless of MDR decisions.

## The Political Dimension

FM Sitharaman's clarification that consumers will not be charged for UPI was significant precisely because it was framed as a political commitment, not just a policy position. UPI is one of the government's most popular digital public infrastructure achievements, and charging consumers would be politically difficult.

But the legislation creates the framework for the NPCI's Steering Committee to make MDR decisions that don't directly touch consumers. The question is whether MDR would be imposed on merchants, which could be more politically palatable but would still affect the ecosystem.

## The Bottom Line

UPI's future remains secure for consumers. The government has made clear that the political will to charge users doesn't exist. But the legal framework has now shifted in a way that makes future changes possible, even if they don't happen soon.

For Indian fintech startups, the message is clear: build businesses that thrive within the current free-UPI model, but design them with enough flexibility that they can adapt if the rules change. The companies that prepare for this now will be in a much stronger position than those that assume the status quo is permanent.

The beauty of UPI is that it works at massive scale for everyone. Its challenge going forward will be making that scale economically sustainable without breaking the model that made it successful.
